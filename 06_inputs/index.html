<div class="textcontainer"> <p class="margin"> </p> <h1>➡️ Week 6: Electronic Inputs ⬅️</h1> <br></br> <h2>Part I: Make and calibrate a capacitive sensor</h2> <br></br> <p class="p1">For this week&rsquo;s assignment, I experimented with building a simple proximity sensor. I began by laser-cutting a plywood plank, covering its surface with conductive copper tape, and attaching a wire to one end. That wire served as the sensor input, which I connected to the breadboard along with a 3 M&Omega; resistor and a sense wire.</p> <img src="IMG_8740.jpeg" alt="setup" class="center"> <p style="text-align: center;"><em>Capactive sensor wiring</em></p> <p class="p1">After writing a basic Arduino sketch to measure proximity, I ran into an issue where the serial monitor only returned values of 1 or 2. I tried several troubleshooting steps&mdash;shifting the pins, grounding the back of the wood plank, and adjusting my wiring&mdash;but the biggest improvement came from increasing resistance. By placing three resistors in series for a total of 9 M&Omega;, the sensor began responding reliably.</p> <img src="IMG_8739.jpeg" alt="setup" class="center"> <p style="text-align: center;"><em>My extremely professional calibration setup ...</em></p> <p class="p1">To calibrate, I measured the distance between my hand and the sensor while slowly moving closer. The resulting plot shows that the sensor has limited sensitivity at longer distances (1–10 cm) but becomes much more responsive upon direct contact. This makes sense given that the capacitive effect increases dramatically when a conductive object is very close or touching the plate, causing a sharper change in the sensed value.</p> <img src="Graph1.png" alt="graph1" class="center"> <p class="margin"> </p> <div class="flexrow"> <a id="btn" href="Capacitive Sensor.zip" download>Capacitive Sensor Code </a> </div> <p class="margin"> </p> <br></br> <h2>Part II: Configure and calibrate another sensor</h2> <p class="p1">For my second sensor, I took inspiration from my sister&rsquo;s <a href="https://allisonktu.github.io/PS70/06_week6/index.html">knitting-enclosed capacitive-sensing assignment</a> and built a Velostat pressure sensor. I attached one piece of copper tape to each side of a square of Velostat and then taped the entire &ldquo;sandwich&rdquo; onto a laser-cut wooden plate to make it easier to press consistently.</p> <img src="IMG_8741.jpeg" alt="setup" class="center"> <p style="text-align: center;"><em>Velostat wiring</em></p> <p class="p1">I didn&rsquo;t have standardized weights in the lab, but I did find resin-mixing cups with volume markings along the side. I used these as a proxy for controlled, incremental loads by filling the cup with increasing amounts of water and placing it on the sensor surface.</p> <img src="IMG_8742.jpeg" alt="setup" class="center"> <p style="text-align: center;"><em>Another ultra-professional calibration set-up</em></p> <p class="p1">The calibration results showed a familiar pattern for piezoresistive materials like Velostat. At low pressure, the resistance remains relatively high and stable, so the readings barely change. As pressure increases, the conductive particles inside the Velostat compress and come into closer contact, causing a sharp drop in resistance and a corresponding steep rise in the sensor output. At higher pressures, however, the material approaches a saturation point where additional compression produces diminishing electrical changes, resulting in the flattened region of the curve. This non-linear response is characteristic of Velostat and helps explain why the sensor is most informative within a mid-range band of applied pressure.</p> <img src="Graph2.png" alt="graph2" class="center"> <p class="margin"> </p> <div class="flexrow"> <a id="btn" href="VelostatCode.zip" download>Velostat Code </a> </div> <p class="margin"> </p> </div>
